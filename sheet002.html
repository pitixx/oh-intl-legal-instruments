1 3 International Plant Protection Convention (IPPC) 185 1951, 1997 Commission on Phytosanitary Measures (FAO) The IPPC aims to prevent the introduction and spread of plant pests across borders while facilitating safe trade. It establishes standards for phytosanitary measures, encourages harmonization across countries, and creates a framework for international surveillance, certification, and response. Through national plant protection organizations (NPPOs), regional bodies, and the Commission on Phytosanitary Measures, the IPPC coordinates efforts to protect plant health, food security, biodiversity, and trade. The 1997 revision emphasizes science-based measures, risk analysis, and the One Health relevance of preventing plant pest outbreaks that can destabilize ecosystems. 1, 2, 4, 6
The Convention strengthens national plant health systems and surveillance, building foundational capacity for pest detection and response (1); prevents the international spread of pests that can affect food security and ecological balance, through early warning and border control (2); establishes global standards for phytosanitary certification, supporting safe trade and reducing plant-borne food safety risks (4); and links plant protection with broader environmental health by preventing the destabilization of ecosystems from invasive pests and plant disease outbreaks (6).		 1, 3, 4, 5
1: Reinforces equitable responsibilities across countries through harmonized plant health measures.
3: Aims to preserve ecological balance by preventing the spread of invasive plant pests.
4: Emphasizes prevention over reaction, promoting long-term environmental stewardship.
5: Facilitates cross-sectoral and intergovernmental collaboration through regional organizations and international standards.		 Legally binding framework for coordinated global plant health governance: The IPPC requires countries to establish national plant protection organizations (NPPOs) and adopt science-based phytosanitary measures, creating an institutional backbone for coordinated, rules-based pest management.
Harmonized international standards and surveillance systems: Through the Commission on Phytosanitary Measures, the IPPC enables the development of global standards (ISPMs) and promotes pest surveillance and risk analysis that underpin early detection and cross-border response.
Integration of plant health with trade, food security, and environmental protection: The treaty recognizes that protecting plants also safeguards ecosystems, food systems, and trade, embedding environmental and economic stability into One Health-compatible governance.
No binding obligations on environmental monitoring or climate-related plant risks: The treaty emphasizes phytosanitary measures related to trade but does not require parties to monitor or address broader environmental drivers of plant health, such as biodiversity loss or land-use change.
Collaboration with other One Health instruments is not formally structured: While the IPPC references cooperation and technical assistance, it does not establish mechanisms to align activities with frameworks related to AMR, zoonotic disease surveillance, or chemical safety, reflecting its pre-One Health era design.		 Preamble: "Taking into account internationally approved principles governing the protection of plant, human and animal health, and the environment…"

Article VIII(1): "The contracting parties shall cooperate with one another to the fullest practicable extent... particularly in the exchange of information on plant pests, especially the reporting of the occurrence, outbreak or spread of pests that may be of immediate or potential danger, and in participating in special campaigns for combatting pests that may seriously threaten crop production and need international action."

Article XX: “The contracting parties agree to promote the provision of technical assistance to contracting parties, especially those that are developing contracting parties, either bilaterally or through the appropriate international organizations, with the objective of facilitating the implementation of this Convention.”		 WTO Agreement on Sanitary and Phytosanitary Measures (SPS) as recognized international standards

6 2 United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) 168 1982; 1994 Meeting of States Parties (UN) Establishes a comprehensive legal framework governing all uses of the world's oceans and seas. Defines the rights and duties of states over maritime zones, regulates the exploitation of marine living resources, and imposes binding obligations on states to protect and preserve the marine environment from all sources of pollution. Part XII creates a global architecture for marine environmental protection, requiring states to prevent, reduce, and control pollution; monitor environmental risks; conduct environmental impact assessments; and cooperate internationally to harmonize rules and standards. Part V and Part VII govern conservation and sustainable use of living marine resources in exclusive economic zones and the high seas respectively.  1, 2, 4, 6
Builds institutional and cross-state capacity for marine environmental monitoring and pollution response (1); addresses risks from marine pollution and ecosystem degradation that can drive disease emergence at the human-animal-ocean interface (2); regulates living marine resources and aquatic food systems with direct implications for aquatic food safety (4); and integrates the marine environment into international governance through binding obligations on pollution prevention and environmental impact assessment (6).		 1, 3, 4, 5
1: Affirms equitable sharing of ocean resources and recognizes the special requirements of developing states in accessing marine resources and building monitoring capacity (Articles 61–62, 119).
3: Requires conservation measures to maintain populations of harvested species and  dependent species, and to protect rare or fragile ecosystems and threatened marine life (Articles 61(4), 194(5)).
4: Imposes a binding duty on all states to protect and preserve the marine environment (Article 192), embedding long-term stewardship as a legal obligation.
5: Mandates global and regional cooperation among states and through competent international organizations to develop harmonized environmental rules and standards (Article 197).		 Binding legal framework for marine environmental protection: Part XII imposes enforceable obligations on states to prevent all forms of marine pollution and to protect rare and fragile marine ecosystems (Articles 192, 194, 207). This creates a legal baseline for aquatic and coastal ecosystem health directly relevant to One Health.
Mandatory monitoring and transboundary notification: Articles 204–206 require states to monitor pollution risks, assess potential environmental effects of planned activities, and publish findings; Article 198 requires immediate notification to other states when the marine environment faces imminent pollution damage, establishing early-warning obligations analogous to those in the IHR for human health.
Conservation of aquatic living resources as a shared responsibility: Articles 61, 62, and 119 establish science-based conservation and data-sharing obligations for marine living resources across EEZs and the high seas.		 Limited direct integration with human or animal health systems: UNCLOS was designed as an oceans governance instrument; its One Health relevance must be drawn through interpretation and linkage to other instruments.
Enforcement dependent on flag state compliance: Many of UNCLOS's pollution prevention obligations rely on flag state enforcement, which could create gaps particularly relevant to pollution affecting fisheries and coastal food systems. 		 Article 192: States have the obligation to protect and preserve the marine environment.

Article 194(5): Measures taken under Part XII shall include those necessary to protect and preserve rare or fragile ecosystems as well as the habitat of depleted, threatened or endangered species and other forms of marine life.

Article 196(1): States shall take all measures necessary to prevent, reduce and control pollution of the marine environment resulting from the intentional or accidental introduction of species, alien or new, to a particular part of the marine environment, which may cause significant and harmful changes thereto.

Article 197: States shall cooperate on a global basis and, as appropriate, on a regional basis… in formulating and elaborating international rules, standards and recommended practices and procedures… for the protection and preservation of the marine environment.		 Aquatic Animal Health Code

11 3 United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) 198 1992, 1994 Conference of the Parties The Convention on Climate Change sets an overall framework for intergovernmental efforts to tackle the challenge posed by climate change. It recognizes that the climate system is a shared resource whose stability can be affected by industrial and other emissions of carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases. On this basis, the Convention commits Parties to cooperate in limiting greenhouse gas emissions and in developing responses to climate impacts, particularly in ways that protect vulnerable populations and support sustainable development. 2, 3, 6
It is the primary treaty addressing climate change as an environment and health issue. UNFCCC frames climate change as causing “adverse effects” on natural and human systems and obligates Parties to anticipate, prevent, or minimize those effects (Arts. 1 and 3) (2), requires Parties to develop and implement adaptation measures to reduce vulnerability to climate impacts, which explicitly include impacts on human health like disease (3), and is built on protecting the climate system in light of environmental commitments (Preamble) (6).		 1, 2, 3, 4, 5

2: Common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities (Art. 3) explicitly recognizes inequality among states
3: Recognizes adverse effects on ecosystems and biodiversity and treats environmental stability as essential to human well-being (Arts. 1, 2)
4: Explicitly calls on Parties to protect the climate system for the benefit of present and future generations (Art. 3)

15 3 Rotterdam Convention on the Prior Informed Consent Procedure for Certain Hazardous Chemicals and Pesticides in International Trade 157 1998, 2004 Conference of the Parties (UNEP/FAO)  The Rotterdam Convention promotes shared responsibility and cooperative efforts among countries in the international trade of certain hazardous chemicals and pesticides. It establishes a Prior Informed Consent (PIC) procedure, requiring exporters to obtain consent from importing countries before shipping listed substances. The Convention enhances transparency, reduces health and environmental risks from toxic exposures, and strengthens regulatory capacity—particularly in low- and middle-income countries—through information sharing and technical support. 4, 6, 1, 2
The Convention improves food safety by requiring countries to assess and consent to the import of hazardous pesticides and industrial chemicals that may contaminate food and feed supplies (4); integrates environmental, human, and animal health protection into global trade by regulating cross-border chemical flows that may threaten ecosystems and biodiversity (6); enhances institutional and regulatory capacities in chemical risk management, particularly for low-resource countries, through shared protocols (1); and reduces indirect drivers of zoonotic and chronic disease by limiting environmental toxicant exposure (2).

1, 3, 4, 5

3: Supports socio-ecological equilibrium by reducing the flow of pesticides and industrial pollutants that destabilize ecosystems.
4: Advances environmental stewardship through the precautionary management of toxic substances and the obligation to share risk information transparently.
5: Fosters transdisciplinary and multisectoral collaboration between agriculture, health, trade, and environmental regulators through joint implementation.		 Empowers countries to prevent harmful chemical exposures across humans, animals, and ecosystems: The Convention enables importing countries to reject or restrict chemicals that threaten environmental integrity, livestock health, and community well-being, reducing multi-species exposure to toxins.

Mandates transparent, science-based decision-making in international trade: It requires that hazard assessments and risk communications be shared with all parties, enhancing One Health-aligned accountability in cross-border pesticide and industrial chemical flows. Processes for listing substances may involve multiple steps: Differences in national positions can affect the time required to reach decisions.
Non-compliance challenges may arise in some contexts due to differing national enforcement capacities. Article 10 – Obligations in Relation to Imports of Chemicals Listed in Annex III: Requires each party to decide whether to allow imports of listed hazardous substances and to communicate those decisions transparently, empowering national governments to manage cross-sector risks to food safety, environmental health, and community well-being.

16 3 Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants (POPs) 152 2001, 2004 Conference of the Parties (UNEP) The treaty protects human health and the environment from chemicals that persist in the environment, bioaccumulate through the food web, and pose risks of causing adverse effects. It aims to eliminate or restrict the production, use, and release of specific POPs, many of which are linked to serious health issues, including cancer, immune system disruption, and reproductive disorders, by controlling their trade, disposal, and emissions. 1, 2, 4, 6
The Convention strengthens national and regional capacities for chemical monitoring, regulatory oversight, and pollution-related health risk assessment (1); mitigates environmental degradation that increases vulnerability to zoonotic disease emergence and immune suppression in wildlife and humans (2); reduces long-term foodborne and feed borne exposure to toxic chemicals by phasing out persistent organic pollutants from agricultural and industrial processes (4); and directly integrates environmental protection into global health through legally binding controls on ecosystem-contaminating substances (6).		 1, 3, 4, 5
1: Promotes equity between sectors by requiring international cooperation, technical assistance, and capacity building to support low- and middle-income countries in managing hazardous pollutants
3: Aims to restore socio-ecological equilibrium by eliminating toxic substances that disrupt ecosystems, biodiversity, and the health of humans and animals
4: Embodies environmental stewardship through precautionary measures and lifecycle approaches to chemical use and waste
5: Reinforces transdisciplinary collaboration across environmental science, toxicology, public health, agriculture, and trade sectors (5).		 Links ecosystem health to human and animal health through binding controls on pollutants: The Convention targets chemicals that bioaccumulate and disrupt endocrine, reproductive, and immune systems across species, reinforcing the shared health risks of environmental contamination.
Strengthens national capacity for chemical risk governance: It requires countries to develop national implementation plans and adopt legal and technical measures that bridge environmental protection with public and veterinary health objectives.
Drives international cooperation and resource mobilization for pollution control: Through mechanisms for financial assistance, technology transfer, and capacity building, the Convention enables low-resource countries to participate in pollution control efforts with One Health relevance.		 Sectoral focus limits explicit One Health integration: While environmental contaminants can affect health, the treaty does not formally link its implementation to coordinated health-sector strategies.
National implementation varies: Differences in monitoring capacity and regulatory systems influence consistent application. Cross-sector coordination mechanisms are not explicitly defined within the treaty text.		 Article 3 – Measures to Reduce or Eliminate Releases from Intentional Production and Use: Bans or restricts highly toxic chemicals like DDT and PCBs that bioaccumulate across food chains, causing immune and developmental harm in wildlife, livestock, and humans, especially in vulnerable communities and ecosystems.

Article 5 – Measures to Reduce or Eliminate Releases from Unintentional Production: Targets the release of POPs from industrial combustion and open burning, which contaminate air, soil, and water, elevating risks of chronic disease, cancer, and reproductive failure across species and disrupting ecological health in rural and peri-urban zones.

Article 11 – Research, Development and Monitoring: Promotes collaborative research on POPs exposure pathways, effects, and alternatives—opening space for integrated environmental-health surveillance, toxicology, and early warning systems that bridge animal, environmental, and public health data.
