2 2 Rio Declaration on Environment and Development 1992 UN Establishes 27 principles to guide states toward sustainable development, integrating environmental protection as inseparable from development. Affirms states' sovereign rights over their own natural resources alongside their responsibility to prevent transboundary environmental harm. Introduces foundational principles, including the precautionary approach, polluter pays, environmental impact assessment, and common but differentiated responsibilities, that have since shaped international environmental and health governance. 1, 6
Principle 9 calls for cooperation to strengthen capacity-building through scientific exchange and technology transfer, supporting health system development (1). The Declaration also frames environmental protection as interlinked with development (Principle 4), promotes ecosystem integrity and partnership for conservation (Principle 7), and emphasizes precautionary approaches to prevent environmental degradation (Principle 15), all foundational to integrating the environment into One Health (6).		 1, 2, 3, 4, 5
1: Affirms common but differentiated responsibilities and special priority for developing countries (Principles 6–7).
2: Recognizes the essential role of women, youth, and Indigenous peoples and local communities in environmental management (Principles 20–22).
3: Frames sustainable development as requiring harmony between humans and nature, and between present and future generations (Principles 1, 3).
4: Calls on states to reduce unsustainable production and consumption patterns and to act as stewards of the Earth's ecosystem (Principles 7–8).
5: Promotes international cooperation and cross-sectoral collaboration on environmental issues (Principles 9–10).		 Normative foundation for environment-health integration: The precautionary principle (Principle 15) and the framing of environmental protection as integral to development (Principle 4) underpin how One Health instruments approach uncertainty and ecosystem risk.
Common but differentiated responsibilities (Principle 7): Provides an equity framework that One Health governance mechanisms draw on when addressing disparities in surveillance, response capacity, and environmental burden between high- and low-income countries.
Transboundary notification and cooperation (Principles 18–19): Establishes early notification obligations for environmental emergencies and harmful transboundary effects, creating a governance precedent for the cross-border disease and environmental alert systems that One Health depends on.		 Non-binding and declaratory: The Rio Declaration is a statement of political commitment, not a legally enforceable treaty. Its principles carry normative weight and have influenced subsequent agreements, but states face no formal accountability mechanism for non-compliance.
Limited direct health framing: While Principle 1 affirms the right to a healthy life, the Declaration is dependent on interpretation through subsequent instruments. The Declaration's impact on One Health is channeled primarily through treaties and strategies adopted after 1992 (e.g., CBD, UNFCCC, Sendai Framework) rather than through its own operational mechanisms.		 Principle 1: Human beings are entitled to a healthy and productive life in harmony with nature.

Principle 4: Environmental protection shall constitute an integral part of the development process and cannot be considered in isolation from it.

Principle 7: States shall cooperate in a spirit of global partnership to conserve, protect and restore the health and integrity of the Earth's ecosystem.

4 3 The International Code of Conduct
on Pesticide Management		 2014 FAO/WHO Provides a voluntary framework for all public and private entities engaged in or associated with the management of pesticides throughout their lifecycle, from production and formulation to distribution, use, and disposal. This version broadened the scope of the Code beyond agricultural pesticides to incorporate public health pesticides and vector control, giving greater attention to health and environmental aspects. Describes the shared responsibility of governments, pesticide industry, and civil society to ensure that the benefits of pesticides are achieved without significant adverse effects on human and animal health or the environment. It promotes Integrated Pest Management (IPM) and Integrated Vector Management (IVM), calls for the identification and, where necessary, removal from use of highly hazardous pesticides, and gives particular attention to vulnerable groups including children, women, and people affected by HIV/AIDS. 1, 2, 3, 4, 6
Calls on governments to build capacity in developing countries and strengthen national infrastructure for pesticide management throughout the full lifecycle (1); promotes practices that minimize potential health and environmental risks from pesticides for humans and animals, including through IPM/IVM (2); addresses integrated vector management for control of vector-borne diseases and requires governments to carry out health surveillance (3); calls for pesticides to be used in accordance with Good Agricultural Practice and Codex Alimentarius maximum residue limits (4); and promotes protection of biodiversity and minimization of adverse effects of pesticides on water, soil, air, and non-target organisms (6).		 1, 2, 3, 4, 5
1: Describes the shared responsibility of sectors and promotes equitable access to safe pesticide management, with particular attention to developing countries lacking adequate regulatory capacity.
2: Explicitly identifies vulnerable groups, including children, pregnant and nursing women, infants, the elderly, and HIV/AIDS-affected people, and calls for legislative protection of children from pesticide exposure under the ILO.
3: Calls for protection of biodiversity and minimization of adverse effects on the environment, and promotes IPM/IVM to encourage natural pest control mechanisms and reduce disruption to agroecosystems.
4: Embeds stewardship through a lifecycle approach to pesticide management and promotes product stewardship whereby manufacturers retain responsibility for their products.
5: Calls for coordinated action across governments, industry, international organizations, extension services, farmers, public health workers, environmental groups, and consumer groups, and promotes collaboration between exporting and importing countries.		 Establishes shared responsibilities across sectors for managing a major class of chemical risks to human, animal, and environmental health: The Code explicitly frames pesticide management as a shared responsibility requiring coordinated action by governments, industry, health services, agricultural extension, and civil society, covering every aspect of the pesticide lifecycle.
Strengthens national regulatory systems and surveillance capacity: Governments are called upon to establish pesticide registration schemes, re-evaluation procedures, poisoning surveillance programmes, poison control centres, and residue monitoring systems, creating institutional infrastructure relevant to One Health surveillance.
Promotes IPM and IVM as cross-sectoral approaches to reducing pesticide dependence: Advances integrated approaches that simultaneously protect crop production, human health, animal health, and environmental integrity.		 Variable implementation across countries: The Code specifically acknowledges that many countries have not yet established adequate regulatory schemes, and notes that its standards are particularly important in those contexts, reflecting an uneven baseline for implementation.
Monitoring relies on self-reporting: Governments, industry, and NGOs are invited to report on observance to FAO, WHO, and UNEP Directors-General, but there is no formal compliance mechanism or independent verification system.		 Article 1.4 – Shared Responsibility: States that the Code describes the shared responsibility of many sectors of society to work together so that the benefits of pesticides are achieved without significant adverse effects on human and animal health and the environment.

Article 5.1 – Reducing Health and Environmental Risks: Requires governments to implement pesticide policy and registration systems, carry out health surveillance of those occupationally exposed, provide guidance to health workers on diagnosis and treatment of pesticide poisoning, establish poisoning information centres, and monitor pesticide residues in food, feed, drinking water, and the environment.

Article 3.12 – Resistance Management: Calls on governments, pesticide industry, and international organizations to develop and promote strategies to prevent and manage pest resistance to pesticides, explicitly noting the need to consider the impacts of pesticides used in agriculture on resistance development among disease vectors and public health pests. 10 2 Codex General Standard for Contaminants and Toxins in Food and Feed (CXS 193-1995, Revised 2023) 2023 Joint FAO/WHO Codex Alimentarius Commission The Codex Alimentarius is a collection of internationally adopted food standards and related texts. This code is part of a larger sub-compendium of Codex texts intended to support governments in designing and running a successful strategy to tackle foodborne AMR. This Codex standard that sets out the general principles for managing chemical contaminants in foods and animal feeds and provides a list of maximum levels (MLs) for many important contaminants and natural toxins. Its scope includes environmental contaminants (like heavy metals), naturally occurring toxins (like mycotoxins), and other unintended chemicals in food/feed. Aims to protect public health and ensure fair trade by keeping contaminant levels in food and feed as low as reasonably achievable​. 1, 4, 6
Concerned with contaminants often originating from environmental pollution (air, water, soil) that impact food safety; strengthens risk assessment and management of chemical hazards in the food supply; and promotes integrated food safety by including animal feed.		 2, 4, 5
2: Addresses environmental contaminants in food production.
4: Promotes risk management to prevent chemical
5: Integrates environmental, agricultural, and food safety sciences.		 Collaboration: Among food safety, agriculture, and environmental authorities. For example, to enforce heavy metal limits in food, governments may need to control environmental emissions and ensure farms are not in polluted areas – linking environment ministries with food regulators. The standard’s joint focus on food and feed means veterinary and food agencies must coordinate (e.g. preventing mycotoxin-contaminated feed from reaching animals).
Facilitates trade and information exchange: Provides a unified framework that countries use to harmonize their contaminant limits, facilitating global trade and information exchange on contamination incidents. Harmonization encourages countries to develop coherent policies across sectors (such as agricultural practices, food processing, and waste management) to meet the safety benchmarks.		 Need for extensive monitoring and inter-sector cooperation: Detection and control of contaminants requires environmental monitoring (for pollutants in water, soil), agricultural interventions (good practices to prevent mold toxins or uptake of pollutants by crops), and food inspection. If environmental regulations are weak, contaminants may continue to enter the food chain, undermining the standard.
Gap of scope: Doesn’t cover pesticide residues or veterinary drugs (handled by other Codex standards)​, which means countries must integrate multiple standards – potential silos in these areas might complicate a truly One Health approach.
Resource limitations: in testing for a wide array of contaminants in both food and feed also pose difficulties for low-resource settings.		 Section 1.3: Scope – Applies to contaminants in food and feed that impact human health, including heavy metals and mycotoxins.

Section 3: Maximum Levels (MLs) – Establishes limits for contaminants like lead, aflatoxins, and mercury to prevent toxic exposures.		 WTO SPS Agreement

14 2 MRLs and Risk Management Recommendations for Residues of Veterinary Drugs in Foods (CXM 2-2023) 2023 Joint FAO/WHO Codex Alimentarius Commission Compendium of standards for chemical residues in food from veterinary drugs. It lists the maximum residue limits (MRLs) – the highest levels of specific veterinary drug residues permitted in foods (meat, milk, eggs, etc.) – that are considered safe for consumers, based on scientific risk assessments. It also includes risk management recommendations (RMRs) for certain drugs (e.g. when no safe level can be determined). Helps countries regulate veterinary drug use and ensure that animal-derived foods are chemically safe for human consumption. 4, 5, 6
Strengthens the management of chemical food safety hazards; many veterinary drugs are antimicrobials, and enforcing withdrawal periods and MRLs encourages responsible use, complementing efforts to curb AMR; and addresses pollutants (veterinary chemicals) entering the food chain, a concern highlighted in One Health environmental integration​.		 2, 4, 5
2: Links veterinary drug use to environmental safety.
4: Enforces responsible veterinary drug practices.
5: Requires cooperation between veterinary and food safety regulators.		 Harmonized international benchmark: encourages regulatory cooperation. Food safety authorities and veterinary drug regulators must work together – the former rely on the standard to set national residue limits and testing programs, while the latter control drug use on farms to meet those limits.
Surveillance and testing: Coordination (often through national residue control plans) is a One Health governance success, requiring joint surveillance, data sharing, and cross-agency enforcement. The Codex MRL list, developed with global scientific input (JECFA evaluations), helps countries align their policies, thus smoothing trade and encouraging all countries to build capacity for residue monitoring and laboratory testing.		 Capacity and regulatory: Countries with limited laboratory or monitoring capacity may struggle to enforce MRLs, leading to gaps in consumer protection and animal drug oversight. Another challenge is ensuring veterinary and food safety authorities coordinate effectively – if veterinary services do not enforce prudent use or if farmers are unaware of withdrawal requirements, residues can still end up in food above the limits.
Focus on consumers:  focuses on consumer health; it does not directly address environmental residues or occupational exposure, which means additional measures (outside Codex) are needed to fully protect ecosystems and farm workers.
Finite list: Codex MRLs exist for a finite list of drugs – national authorities must manage risks from veterinary chemicals not yet evaluated by Codex, which can complicate a unified One Health strategy. 16 3 Provisional Voluntary Code of Conduct on the Sustainable Management of Plastics in Agriculture 2025 FAO Provides guiding principles and measures that governments, manufacturers, and other agrifood system stakeholders may adopt to promote sustainable management practices for plastics used in agriculture. It covers all agricultural sub-sectors, including crops, livestock, fisheries, aquaculture, and forestry, and addresses the full lifecycle of plastics from design and manufacture through to end-of-life management. The Code is organized around seven interconnected goals, including contributing to food security, reducing plastic use that poses risks to the environment or human health, promoting sustainable product design, eliminating leakage into the environment, and supporting equitable participation of affected populations, including smallholder farmers, women, youth, and Indigenous Peoples. 4, 6
Explicitly aims to protect human and animal health, food safety, food security, soil, and water from pollution resulting from the use of plastics in agriculture, and to examine pathways and impacts of plastics, including microplastics, on agroecosystems, food safety, and human health (4); integrates environmental considerations across agrifood systems by addressing plastic pollution's impacts on habitats, ecosystems processes, and biodiversity, and by promoting lifecycle management of agricultural plastics to prevent environmental contamination (6).		 1, 3, 4, 5
1: Calls for fair and inclusive participation of affected populations, with special attention to smallholder farmers, women, youth, Indigenous Peoples, persons with disabilities, and groups in vulnerable situations in rural areas, including waste pickers.
3: Addresses plastic pollution's documented impacts on soil properties, carbon and nutrient cycling, and biodiversity, recognizing the interconnection between environmental integrity and human and animal well-being.
4: Promotes stewardship through the 6R model (Refuse, Redesign, Reduce, Reuse, Recycle, Recover).
5: Encourages multistakeholder collaboration across governments, manufacturers, academia, civil society, and international organizations, and promotes South-South and Triangular Cooperation and cross-sectoral R&D.		 Establishes a cross-sectoral policy framework linking agricultural plastic management to human, animal, and environmental health. Objectives explicitly include protecting human and animal health, food safety, soil, and water from agricultural plastic pollution, providing a governance basis for addressing a diffuse, multi-pathway environmental health risk.
Promotes evidence-building and monitoring to guide decision-making: Countries are encouraged to develop strategies to measure and monitor plastics use and alternatives, and to cooperate on common data collection and reporting approaches, including relevant SDG indicators.
Supports national policy development and technical cooperation: FAO commits to developing specific technical guidelines and manuals to support implementation, and encourages countries to build their own regulatory frameworks with particular support to developing countries, economies in transition, and Small Island Developing States.		 Relationship to a future legally binding instrument: The VCoC was developed in parallel with negotiations for an international legally binding instrument on plastic pollution (INC process); it is intended to complement, not preempt, that process, and its scope and status may evolve depending on the outcome of those negotiations.
Non-binding monitoring: Countries are invited to monitor implementation and report to FAO on a voluntary basis only, with no formal compliance or review mechanism established.		 Article 2.1(ii) – Objectives: States that the VCoC aims to protect human and animal health, food safety, food security, soil, water, and the environment from pollution resulting from the use of plastics in agriculture.

Article 4.6.1(iv) – Research and Development: Calls on stakeholders to examine the pathways and impacts of plastics used in agriculture, including microplastics, on agroecosystems, food safety, and human health.

Article 5.2.2 – Alternatives Assessment: Requires that alternative practices and products be assessed for their potential impact on human, animal, and soil health, agrifood systems, and the environment, considering unintended consequences and trade-offs.
