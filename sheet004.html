1 3 Ramsar Resolution IX.23: Highly pathogenic avian influenza and its consequences for wetland and waterbird conservation and wise use 2005 Conference of the Contracting Parties Recognizes the ecological, health, and livelihood risks posed by the spread of HPAI (H5N1), rejects lethal control of wild birds and wetland habitat destruction, and calls for coordinated, multisectoral response planning and surveillance. It positions wetland integrity as critical to preventing disease spread and stresses scientific data, international cooperation, and ecosystem-based outbreak management. 1, 2, 3, 6
Urges national preparedness, multisector coordination, and contingency planning for zoonotic risk (¶16, ¶21) (1); acknowledges HPAI as a zoonotic risk, emphasizes cross-sectoral response to reduce spillover potential (¶1–3, ¶20) (2); focuses on threats to migratory bird populations and ecosystem health as endemic or epidemic disease pressures mount (¶1, ¶12, ¶15) (3); frames wetland destruction and culling as counterproductive, underscoring the role of intact ecosystems in outbreak containment (¶18, ¶22) (6).		 1, 3, 4, 5

5 3 WOAH Resolution 27: The “One Health” Approach to Address Health Risks at the Animal Human Ecosystem Interface 2012 WOAH The resolution promotes integrated One Health governance by linking human, animal, and ecosystem health through collaboration, strong veterinary services, and global partnerships.
The resolution underscores Veterinary Services' role in preventing zoonoses, tackling antimicrobial resistance, and ensuring food safety, emphasizing intersectoral collaboration and integrated approaches. It highlights strengthening Veterinary Services, harmonizing WOAH and WHO standards, incorporating One Health into education, and using rabies control as a One Health model.		 1, 2, 3, 5, 6
The Resolution promotes integrated capacity building for surveillance, preparedness, and response systems across human, animal, and environmental sectors (1); it aims to mitigate health risks at the animal-human-ecosystem interface, especially those posed by emerging zoonoses and pandemics (2); it supports collaborative control of endemic and neglected zoonotic diseases through multisectoral coordination (3); it encourages prudent antimicrobial use in animals to tackle AMR (5); and it underscores the role of environmental health and ecosystem disruption in disease emergence, supporting an ecological perspective in health risk governance (6).		 1, 3, 4, 5
1: Promotes equity by encouraging cooperation across sectors at the animal-human-environment interface
3: Emphasizes interconnectedness of humans, animals, and ecosystems
4: Calls for responsible stewardship by advocating preventive, sustainable health policies that protect animal welfare
5: Supports transdisciplinary collaboration by urging integrated, multisectoral responses involving veterinary, medical, environmental, and other knowledge systems.
 Institutional coordination mechanisms: Promotes the development of cross-sectoral coordination platforms to align veterinary, public health, and environmental actions under a unified One Health approach.
Risk-based policy design: Encourages evidence-informed strategies that integrate ecological and epidemiological data to prevent and control health risks at the animal-human-environment interface.
Monitoring and surveillance systems: Strengthens integrated surveillance efforts across sectors to detect, assess, and respond to emerging zoonotic threats in real time.		 Fragmented veterinary and environmental integration: Veterinary services often operate independently of environmental health institutions, limiting the resolution’s call for joint action at the animal-human-ecosystem interface.
Challenges for national veterinary services in One Health governance: In many countries, veterinary authorities lack formal mandates or decision-making power in intersectoral platforms, reducing their ability to implement the resolution’s multisectoral goals.
Barriers to integrated surveillance systems: Persistent gaps in interagency data sharing and incompatible surveillance infrastructure across sectors hinder early detection and coordinated response to zoonotic threats.		 para 4: Recommends that (1) the Director General and the OIE Delegates advocate to governments and regional and international organisations for the fundamental role of Veterinary Services in the protection of animal, human and ecosystem health and the application of “One Health” approaches, and (5) the Director General encourage relevant OIE Reference Centres to further develop the “One Health” concept and approaches to animal disease detection, prevention and control, including economic studies, and further develop and provide capacity building programmes on implementing “One Health” approaches.

6 3 Conference Resolution 4/2015: Antimicrobial Resistance 2015 FAO Conference Resolution 4/2015 commits FAO to a central role in the global response to antimicrobial resistance, emphasizing a One Health approach that links agriculture, food systems, and public health. It endorses FAO’s action plan on AMR, urges Member States to develop national AMR strategies covering animal, plant, and environmental health, and calls for multisectoral cooperation with WHO and OIE (now WOAH). The resolution also directs FAO to integrate AMR into its normative work, surveillance systems, capacity building, and technical support, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. 1, 2, 4, 5, 6
The resolution commits FAO to building cross-sectoral capacities to address AMR in agriculture, food systems, and the environment (1); supports early action to reduce AMR-related health threats, including zoonotic and foodborne risks (2); promotes food safety through Codex standards and responsible antimicrobial use in production systems (4); positions AMR as a core priority across FAO’s technical work, driving prudent use, stewardship, and national policy reform (5); and acknowledges the environmental dimensions of AMR, calling for collaboration on waste management and environmental contamination (6).		 1, 3, 4, 5
1: Promotes shared responsibility across agriculture, health, and environmental sectors.
3: Highlights ecological drivers of AMR, including waste and environmental contamination.
4: Encourages sustainable antimicrobial use and long-term behavior change in food systems.
5: Formalizes cross-sector collaboration through the Quadripartite and national action plans.		 Institutional anchoring of AMR within FAO’s mandate: Gives FAO a clear leadership role on AMR in food, agriculture, and the environment, reinforcing its legitimacy in One Health coordination.
Support for multisectoral national action plans: Encourages Members to develop and implement cross-sectoral AMR strategies, promoting whole-of-government approaches.
Integration of AMR into food safety and surveillance systems: Links AMR to Codex, veterinary services, and national monitoring frameworks, enabling aligned standards and data systems. 10 3 Resolution 14: OIE's Engagement in the One Health Global Effort to Control Antimicrobial Resistance 2019 WOAH The resolution strengthens global coordination to tackle antimicrobial resistance (AMR) through a One Health approach, focusing on international collaboration, national action plans, and sustainable investments in AMR efforts. 3: Recognizes AMR as a threat to ecological balance
4: Calls for responsible antimicrobial stewardship, urging behavioral change in veterinary and agricultural practices

Surveillance and data harmonization across sectors: Supports the development of integrated AMR surveillance systems, especially through WOAH’s global database (ANIMUSE), to improve data sharing and coordination across human, animal, and environmental sectors.
Capacity building in veterinary governance: Strengthens veterinary systems through training, guidance, and policy support to implement AMR national action plans and engage effectively in One Health governance structures.		 Limited regulation of antimicrobial use in animal sectors: Many countries lack comprehensive legal frameworks to govern the use of antimicrobials in food-producing animals, undermining efforts to reduce misuse and overuse that fuel resistance.
